# Geertz: preserve behavior in context

Use this reference when an observed act can mean different things depending on
local symbols, relationships, history and participant interpretation.

## Procedure

1. Record the act, setting, sequence, participants and observer position before
   interpreting meaning.
2. Gather participant accounts and local categories. Label these emic
   descriptions rather than silently translating them into analyst terms.
3. Add the historical and institutional context needed to distinguish similar
   acts with different meanings.
4. Build a thick description that connects action to locally relevant layers
   of significance while keeping raw observation visible.
5. State the analyst's etic interpretation separately.
6. Test the interpretation against participant disagreement, negative cases and
   a competing contextual account.
7. Limit transfer to settings whose relevant context has been compared.

## Output

- Thin observation
- Context record
- Emic accounts and local categories
- Etic interpretation
- Competing interpretation and negative cases
- Transfer limits and unresolved meanings

## Cautions

- More detail does not by itself create a better interpretation.
- A vivid case is not a prevalence estimate.
- Participant accounts can conflict and are evidence, not privileged certainty.
- Refuse decontextualized generalization when the setting and local meanings are
  unavailable.

## Primary and methodological sources

- Geertz, C. (1973). “Thick Description: Toward an Interpretive Theory of
  Culture.” In *The Interpretation of Cultures*. Basic Books.
- Geertz, C. (1983). *Local Knowledge: Further Essays in Interpretive
  Anthropology*. Basic Books.
- Ryle, G. (1968). “The Thinking of Thoughts: What Is ‘Le Penseur’ Doing?”
  University Lectures, University of Saskatchewan.
