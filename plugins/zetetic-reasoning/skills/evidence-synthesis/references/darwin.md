# Darwin: accumulate difficulties, not only confirmations

Use this reference when a theory develops across many observations and the
hardest exceptions are more informative than another easy confirmation.

The “difficulty book” below is a modern reconstruction from Darwin's practice
of preserving objections and addressing difficulties. It is not presented as a
named historical protocol.

## Procedure

1. State the theory and what it predicts in the case under review.
2. Maintain a difficulty ledger with the contrary observation, provenance,
   date, predicted result, actual result and current explanation.
3. Rank difficulties by how much they could change the theory, not by how easy
   they are to answer.
4. Investigate the strongest case first. Seek observations from different
   settings and collection paths.
5. Separate an auxiliary-assumption failure from a core-theory failure.
6. Record how each new observation changes the theory, its boundary conditions
   or confidence. Preserve unresolved entries.
7. Present the surviving theory together with the hardest remaining difficulty.

## Output

| Difficulty | Source | Prediction | Observation | Threat level | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Add the current theory, its revised scope, alternative explanations and the
next observation that would discriminate among them.

## Cautions

- An explanation added after every anomaly can make a theory unfalsifiable.
- A memorable exception is not automatically representative; verify frequency
  and selection.
- Long accumulation does not repair biased collection.
- Refuse closure while the strongest well-supported contradiction has no
  adequate account.

## Primary and historical sources

- Darwin, C. (1859). *On the Origin of Species*, especially the chapter
  “Difficulties on Theory.” John Murray.
- Darwin, C. (1837-1838). Notebook B, “Transmutation of species.” Darwin
  Manuscripts Project: https://darwin-online.org.uk/
- Darwin, C. (1871). *The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex*.
  John Murray.
